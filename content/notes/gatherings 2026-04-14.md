There is but a tiny window between desirable light and common light. In my room, a window faces East and welcomes desirable light. I raise the blinders and residues of desirable light gather with shadows of the window on a year-old Jōdo Shinshū calendar gifted to me by a Japanese couple. Underneath an _ukiyo-e_ of strawberries, a _shodō_:

>To live in the true spirit of the Pure Land,
>we need to make Dharma-listening our way of life.

At the edge of Data, the violet flowers of a garlic vine from about four days ago have turned white. An undeniable tint of brown has crept into most of them. I smell no scent here—only the scent of decay.

3

The Church Among the Palms, which I often pass by along Velasco Avenue, was built in 1917, ten years after an American Presbyterian mission began among students and teachers at the university. In the 1920s and 1930s, different species of palms were planted around the chapel, giving it its name. When the Japanese occupied the campus in 1941, they burned the new buildings constructed around the chapel and then turned the chapel into a stable for horses. It wasn’t until 1960 that the first Filipino pastor led the church. It wasn’t until the 1990s that Tagalog was used as a language for worship. It wasn’t until 2024 that a typhoon uprooted one of the oldest palms planted in the church premises, falling and crushing a part of the perimeter wall.

On this spot of Lantican Avenue, as the road begins to ascend, numerous sightings of Maria Makiling descending from the mountain have been reported. They built a statue in a garden near the spot to mark it. This morning, a couple in jogging clothes walk along the spot while a jeep full of passengers belches thick gray smoke as it chugs on the slope. After a few moments, the hum of an electric bike briefly overlaps with her near-silent breathing. She grasps the umbrella in her right hand. She has no use for it now under these trees. In just a few steps, she stops and looks down to where she came from. Seeing that the road is clear, she crosses. In a few minutes, she will be beside a bed in the ward, holding the hand of someone waiting for her.

None of these houses was built with air conditioning in mind. Who would have thought fifty years ago that the world would be this hot even in a campus inside a forest? About a month ago, I gave in and bought an air conditioner, the first time I had one in a house I live in (and I am now in my 30s). Like these houses, my apartment was sketched by architects who knew nothing about global warming. I hired two men to cut some of the metal rails on my window so the unit could fit. Peach-colored leaves surround a tall tree at Arbor Square. I raised my eyes and saw a vine that seemed as old as the tree. Along Forestry Apartments, I saw a house without a wall. Nearly its entire floor was also gone. Like many things here, the forest may have consumed them. What is left of the house is the kitchen—its cabinet and cupboards. I remembered the house we left at Santa Maria and wondered what part of it would first be gone. Like all things there, reticence may have consumed it.
